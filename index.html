Robot Chess Arm

2020 – 2021 · Personal project

A six-axis arm that plays chess on a real board. I extended a PhantomX Reactor kit with an aluminium truss and an extra elbow rotation, driven by Dynamixel servos from a MATLAB app. A RealSense depth camera looks down on the board. Instead of recognising pieces, it works out which squares are occupied and compares that with the last position to infer your move. Stockfish picks the reply.

Full write-up

The arm started as a PhantomX Reactor kit. I extended it to six degrees of freedom with an aluminium truss and an extra elbow rotation, loosely modelled on industrial KUKA arms. It is driven by Dynamixel servos (MX-106, MX-64 and others) over a serial bus. Kinematics use Peter Corke's Robotics Toolbox, and the chess engine is Stockfish, talking UCI. Everything runs from a MATLAB app.

Seeing the pieces

My first attempt used an RGB image and a CNN. It broke whenever the lighting changed. Simple thresholding of the depth image was not reliable either. What worked was to stop trying to recognise pieces. The robot already knows the position, so it only has to work out which squares are occupied, compare that with the previous state, and infer the move.

An Intel RealSense D435 looks down on the board. It projects an infrared speckle pattern so that depth works even on plain surfaces. The depth image becomes a point cloud, the board plane is removed, and the remaining points are clustered into objects, each assigned to a square.

Calibration is one piece placed on the centre of the board. The median of its points gives the centre, and the cloud is clipped to the 30 cm board around it. The first segmentation simply counted points per square, and it failed whenever a piece leaned over two squares. Clustering fixed that: the cloud is flattened, split into clusters at least 5 mm apart, and refined iteratively. A cluster wider than 1.3 squares is split in two with k-means, and clusters stacked close together are fused. The CNN experiments before that used about 600 photos per class.

Moving the pieces

The arm is modelled as six revolute joints in Denavit–Hartenberg form, with joint limits, and inverse kinematics finds the pose for each square. The board centre sits 27.5 cm from the base, and each square is 37 mm. Each piece type has its own pick height, from 30 mm for a pawn to 57 mm for the king. The servos are on a Dynamixel bus at 1 Mbps. The shoulder carries the most load, so it is driven by two servos working as a pair. A MATLAB wrapper speaks UCI to Stockfish 11 and passes it the position as FEN.

Results

I wrote the detection work up as a short paper, comparing segmentation and clustering approaches on 96 board positions. Counting a position as solved only if every square is right, the best method, passive iterative clustering, read 72 of 96 boards perfectly.

72 / 96boards read perfectly
99.1%per-square accuracy
0.76 sper detection

Combined with the move inference, that is reliable enough for the arm to play complete games.

Procedural World

2026 · Personal project

A tool for building 3D game worlds from rules instead of hand-placed models. The world is a graph of pure nodes, and each node’s fingerprint hashes its type, parameters, seed and inputs, so an edit re-cooks only what it touches. Eight packs add ground, cliffs, walls, trees, caves, water, sea life and props, and the renderer is written straight on wgpu. Cliffs are drawn as Bezier curves, caves are dug as distance fields, and walls interlock course by course.

Full write-up

Procedural generation usually means a script that produces a whole level in one go: change one thing and everything is regenerated. I wanted a world you could edit directly, by dragging a wall or digging a cave, and see the result straight away, while still having it generated from rules.

The world is a graph

A world is saved as a directed acyclic graph (DAG) of nodes. A ground box, a cliff curve, a wall path and a scatter of trees are all nodes, and an edge means "this node reads that one's output". Cooking the graph turns it into meshes, textures and instances for the renderer. The whole design rests on one rule: every node is a pure function. Given its type, its parameters, its random seed and the outputs it reads, it always produces exactly the same result, and it touches nothing else. That rule is what makes the rest possible.

1. Every node gets a key

Before anything cooks, each node is given a fingerprint: a hash of its type, its parameters, its seed and the keys of the nodes it reads. Because a node's key contains its inputs' keys, it summarises everything upstream of it, like a Merkle tree, or the way build systems such as Bazel and Nix decide what needs rebuilding.

2. An edit changes keys downstream, and only there

Resize a ground box and its parameters change, so its key changes. That change flows along the edges: every node that reads the box gets a new key, and so does everything that reads those. Nodes upstream or beside the edit keep their keys, because nothing they depend on changed.

3. Same key, same result

The cooker walks the graph in dependency order. If a node's key is already in the cache, its stored result is used as is. Only nodes with new keys cook, and nodes whose inputs are ready cook together in parallel waves. The key itself is the dirty check, so there are no dirty flags that someone could forget to set.

4. Reading less means redoing less

A key is only as fine as what it hashes. If a cliff hashed the whole terrain, every terrain edit anywhere would rebuild every cliff. So a node can declare which part of an input it actually reads, usually a set of terrain tiles, and only the hashes of those tiles go into its key. In the diagram below, the wall reads a tile the edit changed and cooks again, while the cliff reads tiles that stayed the same and keeps its mesh. Large nodes also keep memos inside themselves: the terrain re-lays only the boxes an edit touched.

5. Randomness has to be repeatable

Procedural content is full of random numbers, and caching only works if they come out the same every time. Each node's seed is derived from the graph seed and the node's id, never from the order nodes were added, and ids are never reused. So adding a new tree never changes how the existing ones look. The random hash (PCG-RXS-M-XS) is implemented identically in Rust and in the GPU shaders, so work can move between CPU and GPU and still draw the same numbers.

The cache lives in memory and drops the least recently used results beyond a budget of about 1.5 GB. A saved world also records the revision of every node type and data table it was made with, and refuses to load if a generator has changed underneath it, rather than quietly producing a different world.

Packs

The core knows nothing about walls or caves. That knowledge lives in eight packs: ground, rock and cliffs, stone walls and ruins, trees, caves, water, sea, and a kit of props such as carts, docks, wells and barrels. A pack never depends on another pack. Packs only exchange a small fixed set of data types (height fields, solids, point sets, curves, stones, parts, scatters and trees), and each can carry named layers like a Houdini attribute, which is what lets the output of one pack feed into another. About 160 of the node types are recipes written as data rather than code. Every pack's shaders are checked against every render pass in the test suite.

The terrain is an unbounded, sparse height field made of shared 16 m tiles at 25 cm resolution. Each tile carries its own hash and named layers such as grass, stone and water level. It is shaped from boxes and painted outlines, and the renderer re-bakes only the tiles that changed.

Caves

A cave is dug, not modelled. Rooms (grotto, hall, fissure, pool or cut stone) and tunnels (level, ramp, stairs or ladder) are blended into one signed distance field, roughened with noise and meshed inside out. The dig also cuts the ground open where the cave comes to the surface. Entrances can be a hole or shaft, a door in a cliff, a burial mound or a heap of boulders. Dressing nodes then fill it in: rock chiselled into blocks, dripstone, flowstone and curtains, pools with rimstone terraces, fallen stones settled with physics, mushrooms, crystals and lamps. The dressing stays where it is when the cave is edited upstream.

Walls

Walls are drawn as paths. Where paths meet, the wall pack builds a junction graph: it welds ends, splits T and X crossings, and turns sharp corners into piers. A whole network shares one table of stone courses, and the bond alternates course by course at every junction, so walls interlock the way masonry does. The fill then jitters joints, splits tall blocks and knocks off top stones, and the courses step with the ground. Styles include ashlar, cobble, fieldstone and slate, timber fences, half-timbering, brick and plaster. Windows, arched passages with voussoirs and a keystone, and battlements are cut out of the courses themselves rather than pasted on, and every wall has a decay setting that runs from whole to ruin. Each stone becomes one record on the GPU, drawn instanced from a set of templates.

Props built from parts

Small built things such as barrels, carts, wells, lanterns, furniture and rope come from the kit pack, and they are assembled the way a craftsman would make them. A component node makes one kind of part. kit.staves bends a ring of planks into a barrel's belly, kit.hoop binds an iron band around whatever it is wired onto, and kit.head closes the end with boards. A recipe such as kit.barrel wires those components together. Parts fit each other through sockets and measurements rather than fixed numbers: a hoop measures the staves it binds, and a lid finds the rim of the pot it sits on, so making a barrel taller moves everything with it.

Each part is its own small mesh in its own frame, so the same hoop on a barrel and on a well is one piece drawn instanced. Because the result keeps that structure, a barrel can also come apart: the editor can pull a recipe apart one level at a time, and every component carries a damage hint that says how it breaks. Piles work like physics without a physics engine. kit.pile drops copies one at a time onto a height field of everything already down, tips them over edges, slides them down slopes steeper than their angle of repose and rolls logs into the groove below. A heap of coins is one coin drawn thousands of times.

Ships

A ship is planked from three numbers: length, depth and beam. The hull generator is a port of an earlier one I made in Blender. Bottoms can be round, pointed, hard-chined, flat or hollow, bows pointed, round or hollow, and sterns square, pointed or round. Fullness presets (runner, trader, carrier) and settings for transom, deadrise, tumblehome, stem rake and sheer spring shape it further. The plan, sheer and section curves can be drawn directly on the ship's handle, the same way cliffs are drawn. A keel curve can lift the bottom into arches, and the keel timber, sternpost, rudder, ladders and pump all follow the hull they are fitted to. Decks, hatches, masts, sails and a capstan are kit parts like any other, so one graph can hold a boxy barge, a long elvish runner and a deep dwarvish carrier side by side.

Cliffs drawn as curves

A cliff is drawn, not placed. You drag along the line where its edge should run, and the stroke becomes a smooth Bezier curve through evenly spaced knobs. The cliff is laid along that curve as a chain of pieces, which can be moved, resized and re-jointed afterwards.

Across the curve, the rock face follows a section profile, the way an architect's section drawing cuts through a wall. There are five: vertical, talus, overhang, undercut and ledged. Each is a cubic Bezier path from foot to lip, and a cliff can mix several of them. The design is a port of an earlier cliff script made in Blender.

Pieces are chained end to start into walls of rock, and the section, top and roughness are blended over the joints. Walls whose rock meets are fused into one field. On top of the profile come rock strata that stay level across the whole world, cracks that open along joints, weathered beds and ribs, a rounded lip at the top and a fillet into the ground. The field is meshed on the GPU with surface nets, about 14 compute passes, and a prefix sum keeps the vertex order identical on every run. A CPU copy of the same field is meshed for picking and collision. The rock face mixes 13 stone textures in zones along the cliff. Rubble at the foot is settled and culled entirely on the GPU. A tunnel wired into a cliff opens the rock around it and flares to a portal, although the cliff itself knows nothing about caves.

Rendering

The renderer is written directly on wgpu (Rust's WebGPU) and WGSL rather than on a game engine, because the worlds it draws are unusual: millions of small stones, props and plants that are all generated, not authored. The approach is to do as much as possible on the GPU and as little as possible every frame.

Draw only what changed. An editor spends most of its time showing a still picture. Once the image has settled, the renderer stops drawing. If only the wind is moving, the still parts of the frame are held, and only the vegetation and the water are re-lit. The sun's shadow maps are kept until the light or the scene changes, with a separate set for things that sway.

Let the GPU decide what to draw. Every stone in a wall is a single record on the GPU, instanced from a set of templates. Compute shaders cull every instance against the view, pick a level of detail and write the draw lists, which are drawn with multi-draw indirect, so the CPU never loops over the instances. Occlusion culling runs in two passes. The big solids are drawn first, a depth pyramid (a hierarchical Z-buffer) is built from them, and then the small props, plants and stones are tested against it. A pebble behind a wall is never drawn. Distant foliage turns into impostors, and grass is generated as tufts in four levels of detail that thin with distance without disappearing.

Light once per pixel. It is a deferred renderer. Geometry writes a G-buffer, and a single compute pass then lights each pixel once, however many objects overlap it. Global illumination traces one ray per texel at half resolution, falling back to a baked irradiance volume on weak GPUs. The frame is drawn below screen resolution with a jitter and upscaled with temporal anti-aliasing, and the resolution adjusts itself to keep GPU time within budget. Water is an FFT ocean with a wind sea and two swells in three cascades. There are three quality tiers, the lowest aimed at integrated graphics. Blender served as the reference: I rendered scenes there and matched the real-time output against them.

Trees

There are seven species. Broadleaf crowns are grown by space colonisation (Runions et al., 2007) inside a seeded envelope, bending toward light and drooping under their own weight, and branch thickness follows the pipe model. Conifers grow in tiers of drooping boughs. Trunks flare at the foot into fluted roots that run out over the ground. Leaves are cards on sprays, and a test keeps the wind sway identical between CPU and GPU.

Editor and export

The app is a Bevy shell with egui drawn over the frame. Editing works like Blender: G, S and E to move, scale and raise, X, Y and Z to lock an axis, typed numbers, and one undo step per gesture. Brush strokes are traced back into Bezier outlines, so painted areas stay editable. Worlds save to a versioned package with a recovery checkpoint every minute. They export to glTF files that pass Khronos validation with no errors or warnings, and to tiled, calibrated top-down maps for use in a virtual tabletop. The workspace runs more than 540 tests.

D&D VTT

2023 – present · Personal project

A desktop virtual tabletop for playing D&D online: maps, character sheets, dice, chat, music and combat in one window. It is built from 92 plugins, each its own webpack build loaded at runtime with Module Federation, which will each get their own repository and CI/CD pipeline. Plugins never share state; they send each other intents, such as “roll these dice”. The rules engine knows no particular game, and D&D 5e is one plugin, with more than 20 sourcebooks. The desktop app keeps campaigns in SQLite; only the small Node and mediasoup server, which handles rooms, voice and video, runs in Docker.

Full write-up

The first prototype dates from 2023, and most of the work happened in 2026. The design problem is that a tabletop game is many small programs that all touch the same table: a die roll changes a character's hit points, which ends a spell, which redraws the map. Wiring those together directly turns into a knot. So the project is split into three parts with hard boundaries between them: an Electron desktop app that each player installs, a small multiplayer server, and 92 plugins that hold almost all of the features. A good part of the plugins is sourcebook data written as typed code, so the compiler checks every spell and monster.

Built from plugins

Almost everything is a plugin, loaded at runtime: the dice, the map, the chat, the music player and the D&D 5th edition rules themselves. Plugins declare what they provide and what they need, and the platform refuses to load a set whose dependencies form a cycle. Plugins never share state directly. Each kind of data has one store that owns it, and plugins send each other only intents, such as "roll these dice" or "apply this damage". That rule is what has kept the project workable at this size.

Plugins are separate webpack builds joined with Module Federation. The desktop app is the host container. It exposes a public API and shares one copy of React, MUI, Zustand and the WebRTC client with every plugin, and plugins that draw in 3D also share three.js. Each plugin is its own remote with its own remoteEntry.js, and plugins can import modules from each other: the dice plugin alone exposes about 26. At startup the host reads the manifests, puts the plugins in dependency order, and loads each remote's script into the shared scope. Cross-plugin contracts are typed handles (defineCapability for state and services, defineEvent for announcements), and there are written specs for about 220 capabilities and 130 events.

A build pipeline per plugin

Each plugin has its own package.json, a development and a production webpack config, and a manifest block with its id, category and dependencies. The production build pulls in every dependency of the plugins you select and builds them in tiers, dependencies first. It hashes each plugin's sources and skips any plugin whose hash has not changed, so a change to one plugin rebuilds one plugin. Each build writes a checked plugin.json listing what the plugin uses and provides, and the build fails if a dependency is missing. A custom tsc step then emits the federated TypeScript types, so plugins type-check against each other's exposed modules. In development, any plugin can run either from its last bundle or from a live dev server with hot reload, so only the plugins being worked on need a server.

Today all of this runs locally from one monorepo. The next step is to give every plugin its own repository with its own CI/CD pipeline. On every push, a plugin's pipeline will install it against the published types of the plugins it depends on, type-check it, run its rules tests headlessly, build the production remote, check its plugin.json, and publish a versioned bundle (the remoteEntry.js, its chunks and the manifest) to a plugin registry. The desktop app then only needs the list of plugin versions to load: it resolves their dependencies and fetches the remotes, so a fix to one plugin ships without rebuilding or reinstalling the app. Module Federation is what makes this possible, because a plugin is already a separate build that is joined to the host only at runtime.

Server and multiplayer

Only the server runs in Docker. The desktop app is a normal Electron install on each player's machine, and it keeps its data locally in SQLite. Each campaign is a folder with its own database, opened with better-sqlite3 in Electron's main process in write-ahead-log mode. The schema is a small document store: collections of JSON documents, a table of uploaded assets keyed by their content hash, and a change log. Every write appends to the log, and the main process batches the changes and sends them to the app's windows once per frame in a single message, which is how pop-out windows stay in sync. The server is deliberately small and stateless. It is Node, Express and Socket.IO for rooms and messaging, plus mediasoup as a WebRTC relay (an SFU) for voice and video, so each player uploads their camera once instead of once per player.

The server ships as a two-stage Docker image on Node 22, which mediasoup needs. The base stage installs dependencies straight from the lockfile, including mediasoup's native C++ worker, which needs Python to build, and the production stage compiles the TypeScript. Compose exposes the signalling port and a UDP and TCP port range for media. For a Linux VM there is a second compose file with host networking, because Docker's bridge NAT rewrites the addresses that WebRTC advertises and breaks connection setup. The server has no database: rooms live in memory, and a room's uploads are deleted a while after the last player leaves. That keeps hosting cheap and means no player data sits on a server.

The game master's client is the authority on the rules. A player's action is sent to the game master, whose client runs it through the rules engine and broadcasts the result. Because all rules run on one machine, two players can never disagree about what happened, and the server never needs to understand D&D.

Rules engine

The rules engine does not know any particular game. It has a dice-expression parser and an engine that runs flows. An attack, a spell or a saving throw is a sequence of steps, such as roll, branch, loop, prompt the player, interrupt or change a value, so it can be inspected and interrupted halfway, for example by a reaction. Rules are driven by tags and modifiers rather than special cases in code. D&D 5e is one plugin on top of it, with more than 20 sourcebook plugins and roughly 760 creatures.

The tests live outside the repo and run every spell, feature, monster and item through the engine headlessly. A live tier drives the real Electron app with Playwright. In the last full run the monster flows passed about 7,100 cases and the item and spell tiers several thousand more, with no failures.

Dice

The dice are physically simulated with Rapier and rendered in 3D. The whole throw is simulated ahead of time at 120 steps a second and recorded, and the face that lands is read from the final orientation. The animation then plays back the recording, so every player sees the same roll. Each die is modelled and baked in Blender, and the materials range from wood and brass to elemental dice that crackle, burn or freeze when they land.

Rendering, ships and spells

Rendering uses WebGPU only, through three.js's WebGPU renderer and hand-written WGSL shaders, with no WebGL fallback. All viewers share one GPU device, and PixiJS 8 draws a few 2D layers. The map has fog of war, dynamic lighting with wall occlusion, and weather. Beyond the rulebook there is ship-to-ship combat with crews and cannon arcs, spell effects drawn on the map, an auto-DJ for background music, and importers for maps from other tabletop tools.

Full write-up

Level curves and directional derivatives are easy to define and hard to picture. We wanted students to see them on a real object they could walk around. The question in our report was how AR can help reinforce level curves and directional derivatives in an intuitive, interactive way, framed as carrying maths over into engineering. The player is a government project manager who has to place research buildings on a volcano so that they suffer as little damage as possible in the next eruption. The volcano is a 3D-printed model on the table, and the HoloLens draws the game onto it.

How it plays

The game runs in a loop. You explore the mountain by switching on level curves (with adjustable density), directional-derivative arrows, or steepest-descent paths. You build stations where you think they are safe. Then you simulate the eruption: the lava follows the gradient of the terrain, and you can pause or rewind it to see why a station was hit. The feedback scores you on stations lost and placement height, and there are five levels.

Each level is a planning phase followed by a simulation phase. Planning has no time limit. The derivative arrows answer "where will the lava go?": touching the volcano with the hand ray shows the direction of steepest descent at that point and around it for a few seconds, and several spots can be marked at once. The level curves answer "how fast will it go?": where the rings crowd together the slope is steep, and a slider changes the height between rings live. A virtual copy of the volcano can be laid over the print. In the simulation, buildings hit by lava turn red and stay on the mountain as wrecks, and the lava can be paused and rewound with the tools still in hand. Each surviving building scores by its importance and by its height, which pushes players to build higher up, and every level has a pass mark, so playing it too safe is not enough.

The lava model is deliberately simple. Droplets start at random points around the crater and run downhill by gradient descent over the terrain's height map, with a little inertia. Spheres follow those paths, and as they pass they fill a 3D grid that a marching cubes mesh turns into a lava surface. Rewinding steps back through the recorded grid.

One height map behind everything

The whole game is driven by a single height map of the volcano. The level curves are its contour lines, the arrows are its negative gradient, and the lava runs down that same gradient. So what a student reads off the curves is exactly what the simulation then does. Where the rings crowd together the slope is steep and the lava is fast. The lava always crosses the rings at right angles, because the gradient is perpendicular to the level curves. And it collects in the valleys between ridges, where the curves bend back towards the summit. These are the ideas the course teaches on paper. The game asks students to predict them before the eruption and then shows whether they were right.

What we found

We tested the game in several play sessions with 12 first-year Earth Sciences students. Each got a short briefing, played, and then filled in a survey with open questions and five-point ratings. Eleven of them had never used a HoloLens. Most found the headset overwhelming at first but got used to it, only one found it slightly hard to use, and all but one found the mechanics easy to pick up.

75%said it helped them understand level curves
75%found the build–simulate loop useful
66%said AR improved their learning

Directional derivatives worked less well: about half the students saw no benefit there, and the results for fluid flow were mixed. Part of the reason was practical. The arrows were hard to see, the menus got left behind when players walked around the model, and the HoloLens 2 has a narrow field of view and limited gestures. Our report concluded that the pedagogical benefit was limited: clear for level curves, much less so for derivatives. The sample was also small and from a single faculty. The next steps would be menus that follow the player, clearer arrows, and other real-world scenarios for other parts of the maths curriculum.

Full write-up · Kidney stones

The type of a kidney stone determines how it is treated and how to prevent the next one. The usual way to find out is to remove the stone and send it to a lab. Experienced urologists can often tell by looking at the stone's surface and cross-section through the endoscope, so the question was whether a neural network could learn to do the same.

I fine-tuned ImageNet-pretrained networks (ResNet152V2 and InceptionResNetV2) on endoscopic images of stones, and explored multi-task learning, where one network is trained on several related prediction tasks at once. The image data is clinical and private, so there are no pictures here.

What the experiments showed

The data set was small: about 300 training and 100 validation images at 224 × 224, for a two-class problem. That decided most of the outcome. InceptionResNetV2 trained from scratch reached about 96% accuracy on the training images and stayed at chance, around 50%, on validation, which is overfitting in its plainest form. The pretrained ResNet152V2 did much better. Over 100 epochs its validation accuracy peaked at 89.6% and averaged about 84% over the last ten epochs, while training accuracy reached 100%. Data augmentation barely changed the validation curve. With a validation set of 100 images these numbers are rough, but the gap between pretrained and from-scratch models was clear.

Full write-up · Indoor localization

GPS does not work indoors, but every building is full of WiFi access points, and the signal strength from each one changes as you move. If you record those strengths at known spots (a "fingerprint"), you can later compare a fresh scan against them to estimate where you are.

App 1: activity and room

The first app classifies the user's activity from the accelerometer, using k-nearest neighbours. It cuts the signal into windows of 20 samples and describes each window by its range (largest minus smallest value) on the x, y and z axes, which is enough to tell standing still from walking. The classifier uses k = 7. A second k-NN classifier (k = 5) uses signal strengths from four access points to decide which of four areas the phone is in.

App 2: Bayesian localization

The second app divides the floor into 13 cells. During training it records 30 scans per cell and builds a 100-bin histogram of signal strengths for every access point in every cell. Each histogram is smoothed with a Gaussian kernel, so a strength that never came up in training still gets a small probability instead of zero. To locate the phone, it starts with an even belief across all cells and updates it with Bayes' rule, one access point at a time, until a single cell reaches 90% confidence. Access points that appear in fewer than 80% of scans are ignored, because their absence says more about scan timing than position. A parallel variant scores every access point independently and takes a majority vote, and it gives no answer on a tie. The access point and training logic have unit tests.

Neural Style Transfer

Repainting a photo in the style of another image. We reproduced ST-VAE, a variational method built on a VGG-19 autoencoder, then swapped its backbone for ResNet-50. Full write-up

ST-VAE (Liu et al., ICIP 2021) encodes images with a VGG-19 autoencoder and models style in a learned latent space, so that styles can be mixed and interpolated. Between encoder and decoder sits a variational linear transformation: it describes the style by the covariance of the features and learns a transform that moves the content features toward it. The loss compares Gram matrices through a separate VGG-16. Getting the published code to run took about the first two weeks, because it depended on outdated libraries and the paper did not quite match the code.

Swapping the backbone

We then replaced VGG-19 with a ResNet-50 encoder and a decoder we wrote ourselves. That meant reworking the latent space, the loss and the way the transformation reshapes its tensors. Early fixes that were mathematically valid produced pure noise. There was no time to train on COCO, so the encoder used generic ImageNet weights, and the ResNet version kept only a faint trace of the content image: the outline of a person, the pillars of the library. Some of our changes to the loss were made to get it running rather than from a principled argument, and we came away unsure whether ResNet-50 suits this method at all. It showed us how much ST-VAE relies on VGG's particular features.

Comparing optimisation methods

We also ran three implementations of the classic Gatys-style optimisation on the same stained-glass style: one with Sobel edge regularisation, one with colour preservation, and a patch-based (MRF) variant. Colour preservation gave the best stained glass, with sharp lead lines and flat panes of colour. The comparison was by eye: the lowest numerical loss is not the best-looking image. In hindsight the project was too ambitious for the time and compute we had. Video style transfer with temporal smoothing was the planned next step.

Full write-up · Working with patient databases

Most clinical prediction asks one question: will this patient have the event, and when? Real courses are messier. An ICU patient can go from no breathing support to high-flow oxygen, onto a ventilator, back down again, and then home. A multi-state model describes this directly: a small graph of clinical states, and the probability of moving along each edge at each step.

Three papers came out of this, all built on the same groundwork: large databases of patient records.

Working with large patient databases

The data are electronic health records: the admissions, diagnosis codes, test results and prescriptions a health system logged, linked across hospitals and GP practices. The SAIL Databank holds anonymised records for approximately 13 million people in Wales. MIMIC-IV holds the intensive-care stays of a Boston hospital. Neither is a dataset in the machine-learning sense. They are logs, written to run hospitals rather than for research, and most of the work is turning them into a question a model can answer.

Access

MIMIC-IV is open to credentialed researchers through PhysioNet, after ethics training and under a data use agreement. SAIL is never downloaded at all. A project is approved by its governance panel, and the analysis runs on remote machines inside its secure environment, with only the compute provided there. Anything that leaves, whether a table or a plot, is checked first, and only aggregate results get out. That shapes the research: a model has to train on the hardware inside, and every result has to be planned as an aggregate from the start.

Cohorts and censoring

A cohort is a precise rule for who is in a study and from when: the inclusion criteria, the date follow-up starts, and when each person leaves it. Leaving because the data end or a stay is cut off is censoring, not an outcome, and the models have to treat it that way: the person was event-free up to that point, and nothing is known after. On MIMIC-IV, for example, only each patient's first eligible ICU stay is used, so one person cannot count twice.

From events to states

Records are a stream of events, so they have to be put on a time grid and turned into states. On MIMIC-IV a state is the breathing support for that hour: none, high-flow oxygen, non-invasive or invasive ventilation, with discharge and death as exits. On SAIL it is a band of how many times someone was admitted to hospital in a quarter.

Missing values carry information

Measurements are irregular. A blood test is taken when a doctor is worried, so whether a value exists says something about the patient. Filling the gaps and pretending the table is complete throws that away. All three models instead see the last value, a mask of what was actually measured, and the time since it was measured.

Testing honestly

Data are split by patient, never by row, so no one appears in both training and testing. Where the question is drift, the split is by calendar year instead: train before a cutoff and test after it, the way a model would actually be deployed. Differences between models are tested across folds with corrections for repeated comparisons, because many small gaps between methods turn out to be noise.

TraCeR: competing risks

Some outcomes rule each other out. In intensive care a patient may develop sepsis, die or be discharged, and whichever happens first stops the others from being observed. Modelling each as a separate survival problem gives probabilities that can add up to more than one. TraCeR works in discrete time. For each interval it predicts a score for every cause plus a fixed one for "nothing happens", and a softmax turns them into hazards. The probability of each cause by a given time and the probability of staying event-free then always sum to one, by construction rather than by luck.

The history is fed in as tokens that combine the value, a learned "missing" token and a learned decay with the time since the value was measured. Attention is factorised: first along time within each measurement, then across measurements within each interval. That is far cheaper than attending over every value at every time at once, and it matches how a clinician reads a chart: how a value has moved, then how the values relate. On MIMIC-IV (sepsis, death or discharge, from 24 hours of history) it beat Dynamic-DeepHit on time-dependent AUC and Brier score. The gains on MIMIC-III were smaller, and every method struggled on eICU.

nMSM: states and measurements together

nMSM, the ML4H paper, is a neural multi-state model that forecasts the states and the measurements together. It reads the patient's irregular history: the values, which of them were actually measured, and how long ago. From that it samples the next state, allowing only the transitions the state graph permits, then generates the next measurements given that state, including whether each one would be measured at all, and feeds the completed row back in. Simulated values therefore change later transitions, as they would in reality, without anyone having to write rules for how the measurements evolve. Running the model forward hundreds of times per patient gives both forecasts of how many patients are in each state and full simulated paths, whose transition counts and state durations can be checked against the data.

It was tested on synthetic illness–death data, on 50,873 ICU stays from MIMIC-IV (hourly respiratory support, forecast 48 hours ahead from several points in the stay) and on SAIL. Against six other methods, including multi-state Cox models and SurvNODE, both nMSM variants had the best mean integrated Brier score and time-integrated AUROC. Removing the missingness masks and time gaps hurt the most: when something was measured is information in itself.

APC: calendar drift, age, period and cohort

Records that span ten years also span changes in clinical practice, in coding and in the patients themselves. Epidemiology separates time into three effects: age (how old someone is), period (the calendar year, which picks up changes in practice and coding) and cohort (the year of birth, which picks up generation). The difficulty is that cohort = period − age, so the three cannot be told apart from the data alone: any straight-line trend can be moved from one to another.

This paper asks where that context belongs inside a neural multi-state model. It can be added to each event's representation, used to bias attention towards recent or same-era events, or added as smooth age, period and cohort curves directly to each transition's risk. To make the curves identifiable, only centred age and a single overall drift keep straight-line terms, and the curves themselves are constrained to zero mean and zero slope. That is a statistical choice, not a causal claim.

The variants were tested on simulated populations with known drift, then on SAIL, ELSA (the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing) and MIMIC-IV, training before a cutoff year and testing after it. The answer is that it depends on how the drift arises. When outcome rates shift, adding the context at the output works best. When the meaning of the measurements changes, adding it to the representation or the attention works best. Recalibrating on recent data fixed calibration but not discrimination. On the real data the differences were small, and on SAIL no variant won on every measure. A better forecast also does not mean the learned curves are the true effects of time.
